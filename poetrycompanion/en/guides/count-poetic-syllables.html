How to count poetic syllables in Spanish verse

Counting a verse is not always the same as counting the syllables in each word separately. In poetry, sound connections and the final word can change the meter. This guide covers the basics of counting syllables in Spanish verse.

1. Read the verse aloud

Start by reading naturally and separating the syllables you hear. Poetic meter follows the rhythm of the line, so it can differ from the grammatical syllable count of isolated words.

2. Look for sinalefa

In Spanish poetry, a word ending in a vowel—or in y pronounced as a vowel—followed by a word beginning with a vowel or a silent h followed by a vowel is often joined into one metrical syllable. In «En todo el mar conocido», for example, do el links when read aloud, giving the line eight metrical syllables: En / to / doel / mar / co / no / ci / do.

Sinalefa is common, but rhythm or an expressive pause may lead a poet to keep sounds separate. Listen to the line instead of applying the rule mechanically.

3. Adjust for the verse's final word

  • Add one syllable when the final word is stressed on its last syllable or is a monosyllable.
  • Keep the count unchanged when the final word is stressed on its second-to-last syllable.
  • Subtract one syllable when the final word is stressed on its third-to-last syllable.

For example, «Ya te vas para no volver» has eight syllables when separated, then counts as nine metrical syllables because it ends with the Spanish stressed-final word «volver».

4. Compare the rhythm across lines

Count each line separately and compare the results. A regular pattern can help identify a meter, while a poem may also mix line lengths or use free verse.

Remember: counting is a useful editing aid, but it does not replace reading aloud or the writer's intent.
